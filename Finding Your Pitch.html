A guide to Vocal Pitch Trainer

Sing the note you meant to sing.

Vocal Pitch Trainer listens while you sing and shows you, in real time, how close you are to the note you're aiming for. This guide walks through a first session end to end — what to expect, what the feedback means, and how to get the most out of it.

First, the idea

What "in tune" actually means

Every note you sing is a pitch — a sound wave repeating at a certain speed, measured in hertz (Hz). "In tune" just means your voice's frequency matches the frequency of the note you're going for, closely enough that a listener can't tell the difference.

→

A4and names the nearest note

The app also talks in cents — a finer ruler than the note name alone. There are 100 cents between one note and the next, so a reading of "+12¢" means you're a little sharp (high), and "−30¢" means noticeably flat (low), well before you've drifted a whole note away. Most exercises count you as on pitch within roughly 35–50 cents of the target — close enough that it sounds right, not laboratory-perfect.

Getting set up

Before your first note

Two things are worth doing before you dive in — both take under a minute, and the app will prompt you for the first one automatically.

  1. 1
    Allow the microphone. Your browser will ask once. Nothing you sing is ever uploaded or recorded — it's analyzed live, on your device, and discarded.
  2. 2
    Find your vocal range. Tap "Set range" (or the microphone icon marked Detect in Practice options). Sing your highest comfortable note and hold it briefly, then your lowest — the app suggests a range from the two, which you can nudge before saving.

Once it's set, every generated exercise — scales, arpeggios, sequences — automatically stays inside a range that's comfortable for your voice.

🎧

Before you start singing: find a quiet-ish room, keep the mic a hand's width away, and use headphones if you're playing the reference tone through a speaker — otherwise the mic can pick the tone back up and confuse the reading.

Choosing your path

Two ways to practice

The home screen opens with one choice. Neither is more "advanced" than the other — they're just suited to different moments.

  • G
    Guided lessonsA structured path from ear training up to full patterns. Pick this if you're new, or if you'd rather be told what to work on next than decide yourself.
  • F
    Free PracticeBuild your own exercise from scratch — a handful of tones, a scale, an arpeggio, a sequence. Pick this once you know what you want to drill, or to warm up on something specific before a rehearsal.

Most people move between both in the same session — a guided lesson to learn a skill, then Free Practice to drill it on the notes that matter to them.

Mode 1

Guided lessons: the path

Lessons are grouped into stages, and stages run roughly in order — each one teaches a slightly harder skill than the last. You don't have to track where you are: the home screen always surfaces a "Your next lesson" card with one button, and the full path stays one tap away if you'd rather browse or repeat something.

Three kinds of lesson, three kinds of feedback

The exact screen you see depends on the skill a lesson is training. Here are the three shapes you'll run into most.

Ear training

"Higher or lower?"

No singing yet — just listening. You'll hear two tones and judge which way pitch moved. It feels almost too simple, but it's the skill every other lesson quietly depends on: hearing the difference before you're asked to reproduce it with your voice.

Pitch stability

"Hold it steady"

Sing one note and hold it. Afterwards, a small trace shows exactly what your pitch did during the hold — the shaded band is the on-pitch zone. A flat line through the band (top) is a clean hold; a line that wanders in and out (bottom) is the wobble to work on.

Moving pitch

"Keep moving"

Glide smoothly from one note to another without stopping partway. The dashed line is the straight path between the two notes; the solid line is the shape your voice actually traced. The dot at the end turns red if you land wide of the target — teal if you land on it.

Mode 2

Free Practice: build your own

Free Practice reads top to bottom as five short steps. Each has a small ? next to its heading — tap it any time for a one-line reminder of what that step is for, without leaving the page.

  1. 1
    What to practice Hand-picked tones, or a generated scale, arpeggio, or sequence.
  2. 2
    Exercise setup The controls here change to match what you picked above — notes for Tones, or key / octave / shape for a generated exercise.
  1. 3
    Your exercise What you've built so far, as a row of chips. Drag one to reorder it, or tap × to remove it.
  1. 4
    Practice options Sound, tempo, grading style, and vocal range — how you practice, not what you practice. Tucked away by default so it doesn't compete with the first two steps.
  1. 5
    Play & sing Press play to hear the reference, then sing it back. Your result appears in Results, right below.

Making sense of it

Reading your feedback

While you sing, a horizontal meter tracks your pitch against the target in real time. The needle sits in the middle when you're on pitch, and slides toward "flat" or "sharp" as you drift — colored so you can read your state at a glance, without parsing numbers mid-note.

On pitch — inside the shaded band
Close — just outside it
Off — clearly flat or sharp

The same three colors carry over into every result you've already seen in this guide — the stability traces and the glide trajectory both use them, so once you've learned to read the live meter, the after-the-fact traces read the same way.

Choosing a sound

Practice options lets you pick what plays the reference note: a pure tone (the most neutral reference — nothing to imitate but the pitch itself), piano, or guitar. Piano and guitar give the note some musical context, which some singers find easier to match by ear; pure tone strips that away when you want to test pitch alone.

The long game

Tracking your progress

"My progress" — one tap from the home screen — looks for patterns across everything you've sung so far: a tendency to land flat or sharp, which direction you approach notes from most confidently, which part of your range needs the most attention.

It needs a bit of data before it has anything useful to say — the empty state above is completely normal for your first few sessions. Nothing here ever leaves your device.
